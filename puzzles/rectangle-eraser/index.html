erasing game · corner rectangles · a visual proof

Erase rectangles,
avoid the last square

Both players erase the same board, one corner-pinned rectangle at a time. What survives is a staircase — and its last step is a square nobody wants to erase.

The game. A board of squares. You and the site take turns erasing; a turn erases one rectangle pinned to the board's top-left corner — any size, as long as it covers at least one surviving square. Whoever erases the last square loses. That square is always the bottom-right one: a rectangle that reaches it erases the whole board.

The puzzle. Given the board's shape, who wins — the opener, or the player answering?

Play below — the site never blunders. Try wide boards, tall boards, perfect squares, then flip to the proof.

01 Squares: erase, then mirror

The record under the grid reads one way: the opener wins every board but 1×1. One row is the plainest win: the opener erases everything but the last square.

On a square board the opener erases all but the last row and last column. What survives is two equal arms, meeting at the last square. Every other surviving square sits in a single arm, so every later move erases from a single arm, from the end away from the last square. The opener answers with the same move in the other arm, and the arms are equal again. They run out with the answerer to move — alone with the last square.

Fact 1. On square boards the opener wins: erase all but the last row and column, then mirror.

02 Every board: steal the win

Boards are finite and every move erases at least one square, so no game loops or draws: every board hands one of the two players a forced win — but which?

Suppose the smallest opening — the corner square alone — loses. Then some reply beats it: a rectangle that leaves the opener facing a board with no winning move. But every rectangle covers the corner square, so played as the opening instead, the same rectangle builds the same board — with the answerer now facing it.

Hence one of two openings wins: the corner square, or the reply that would have beaten it.

Fact 2. On every board bigger than 1×1, the opener can force the win.

The argument names no move: it proves a winning opening exists and points at none. One row, two rows, squares — those are solved. Beyond them no rule is known; each board is settled by search, the way the site settles yours.

Which single opening wins every two-row board? And three rows — is there a rule?

The game is Chomp (David Gale, 1974) seen in a mirror: erasing from one corner is eating toward the other. Gale's strategy-stealing argument names the winner without naming a move.
