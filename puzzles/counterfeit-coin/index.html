Twelve coins,
and one of them lies

Identical to the eye, except one — a shade heavier or a shade lighter, nobody says which. All you have is a balance scale, and it only ever says which pan is heavier.

The setup. Twelve coins, identical to the eye. One is fake: a touch heavier or a touch lighter than the rest — you don't know which.

The move. Load coins on the two pans, the same number on each, and weigh. The scale tips to the heavier pan, or balances.

The puzzle. Name the fake with certainty, in as few weighings as possible. How few are always enough? And does thirteen coins change the answer?

01 Twenty-four stories

There are 12 coins, one of them is heavier or lighter than the others. Hence there are 24 possible stories. A story is the fake coin's number, and the direction.

Each weighing ends in one of three answers: the left pan sinks, the right pan sinks, or the scale balances.

A plan says what to load on the pans at each weighing — each choice may depend on the answers so far — and at the end should provide enough information to decide which story is true.

A plan of two weighings can end in at most \(3 \times 3 = 9\) different ways. Since \(9 < 24\), two weighings are not enough. A plan of three weighings can end in \(3^3 = 27\) ways, and \(27 \ge 24\) — so three weighings might be enough.

Fact 1. At least three weighings are necessary.

02 Three bins

Each story determines the answer of any weighing. On a pan, the fake sinks it if heavier and lifts it if lighter; on the bench, it leaves the scale balanced.

Each weighing partitions the possible stories into three bins. The answer keeps one bin and rules out the other two.

After the first weighing, the two weighings that remain can end in at most 9 ways. Hence every bin of the first weighing must hold at most 9 stories — the answer might land there.

03 Twelve words

Now is the time to decide what the weighings will be. Each could depend on the answers before it — but in fact, we will show a single plan that fixes all three in advance.

In each weighing, a coin sits in one of three places: the left pan, the right pan, or the bench — the letters L, R, and B. A coin's places in the three weighings spell its three-letter word:

Suppose coin 8 is heavier. Weighing #1 answers R: coin 8 sinks the right pan. Weighings #2 and #3 answer B: coin 8 waits on the bench, and the pans hold only true coins. The three answers spell RBB — exactly coin 8's word.

Every coin works the same way: a heavier fake spells its own word. A lighter fake tips every weighing the other way — coin 8 lighter spells LBB, its word with L and R swapped: its mirror.

For the plan to work, the 24 stories must spell 24 different answers. Check the list: every row holds four L and four R, so each weighing is 4 v 4, as Fact 2 demands. No two words are equal, so no two heavier fakes spell alike. No word is another's mirror, so a heavier fake never spells like a lighter one — and BBB, the one word that is its own mirror, was left out. Hence all 24 spellings differ, and the three answers you hear name the fake and its direction.

Fact 3. Three weighings suffice for twelve coins — fake and direction — with all three weighings fixed in advance.

04 The thirteenth coin

Now add a thirteenth coin: 26 stories. The plan above never used the word BBB — give it to coin 13, and bench it in all three weighings. Coin 13 heavier spells BBB; coin 13 lighter spells BBB too, since BBB is its own mirror. The other 24 stories spell their answers as before, all different. Hence three balances convict coin 13 — and its direction is never learned: it never sat on a pan.

Every first weighing leaves a bin of ten stories or more, and the two weighings that remain can end in at most 9 ways. So two stories in that bin spell the same answer. On a pan a coin's two stories tip opposite ways, so a heavier-and-lighter pair can spell alike only from the bench — benched to the end, both spelling BB. Now, if the two sharing stories name different coins, the plan cannot even name the fake. If they are one coin's benched pair, the fake is named and the direction is the loss. Either way, no plan can promise the direction of a thirteenth coin.

Fact 4. Thirteen coins: three weighings always name the fake, but no plan can promise its direction.

05 Fourteen, and beyond

Fourteen coins make 28 stories — more than the 27 ways three weighings can end. Two stories must spell the same answer, so no plan learns fake and direction both. Even the name is out of reach: a tipped bin holds one story per pan coin and must stay within 9, so each pan holds at most four coins. Then at least six coins sit on the bench, and the balance bin holds their twelve stories: six pairs. A pair can share only the answer BB, and only one pair can have it. The other five pairs need ten different answers, and only eight remain. Hence fourteen coins need a fourth weighing.

The same counts run for any number of weighings and close at a formula: \(w\) weighings name the fake and its direction among \((3^w - 3)/2\) coins, and one coin more when the direction may stay unknown. The sequence runs \(3, 12, 39, 120, \ldots\) The play bench tops out at 30 coins, well within four weighings.

A last question. Borrow one extra coin that is known genuine. Three weighings can now name the fake among thirteen coins, direction included. Which count does the borrowed coin loosen?

A wartime classic: the twelve-coin problem swept the common rooms of the 1940s fast enough to be blamed, half-seriously, for slowing the war effort.
