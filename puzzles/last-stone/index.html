take-away game · last stone · a visual proof

Take the last stone —
if the pile lets you

One pile, alternating takes, and a single rule that makes every take cap the next. Most piles fall to careful play. Some sizes cannot be beaten.

The game. A pile of \(n\) stones. You and the site take turns removing stones; whoever takes the last stone wins. The opening take can be any number of stones — just not the whole pile. Every later take is at least 1 and at most twice the take before it.

The puzzle. Which pile sizes can the opener always win, and how? Which piles are lost the moment the game starts?

Play below — the site never blunders. Win a few piles as opener, and note the sizes where you can't, before flipping to the proof.

01 The record

The piles where the site cannot be beaten — even when you open — are \(2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, \ldots\) Set a 1 in front of the list, and each number is the sum of the two before it: the Fibonacci numbers.

02 Blocks

What about the piles off the list? Break off the largest Fibonacci number that fits, and repeat: from 20 break 13, from the leftover 7 break 5, and 2 remains. Every pile splits into blocks. A Fibonacci pile is a single block, taken whole at the first break.

A remainder never reaches the Fibonacci number just below the block it left. Watch the 13 broken from 20: a remainder of 8 or more would mean the pile holds \(13 + 8 = 21\), so 21 would have been broken off instead. The remainder is 7, so the next block is at most 5. And 13 is more than twice 5:

\[ 13 = 8 + 5 > 5 + 5 \]

Fact 1. In a split, each block is more than twice the block after it — the blocks are spaced.

Could a pile split into spaced blocks in a second way? Spaced blocks kept below some Fibonacci number can never reach it. Below 13, the top block is at most 8; the next must stay under half of 8 (Fact 1), so it is at most 3; the block after that stays under half of 3, so it is at most 1; and nothing fits under half of 1. The total:

\[ 8 + 3 + 1 = 12 \]

The shortfall nests: \(3 + 1\) falls one short of \(5\), so \(8 + 3 + 1\) falls one short of \(8 + 5 = 13\). Below every Fibonacci number, the tightest packing lands one stone short.

So a spaced arrangement cannot keep all its blocks below the largest Fibonacci number that fits — it would total less than the pile. The top block is forced; then, remainder by remainder, every block is forced.

Fact 2. Spaced blocks that add up to a pile are that pile's split — Zeckendorf's theorem.

03 The winning move

The winning move is to take the whole smallest block. Watch it beat you on the pile of 20, then read why.

Suppose the smallest block is within your cap. Take it whole. If it was the only block, that was the last stone. Otherwise the bigger blocks stand: still spaced, so still the split (Fact 2). The site's cap is twice your take, and the next block up is more than twice your take (Fact 1). The site is left short.

Suppose instead the smallest block is beyond your cap. The last stone is out of reach. Your take comes off the smallest block, and the leftover splits into blocks of its own. Each of them is smaller than the old block — which every bigger block more than doubles (Fact 1) — so the pile stays spaced, and stays the split (Fact 2).

Could the leftover's smallest block be more than twice your take? Split your take into blocks too. Every leftover block would be more than twice the whole take, and so more than twice each of the take's blocks: the two lists together would be spaced. But they add up to the old smallest block — a Fibonacci pile, whose split is a single block, not two or more (Fact 2). So the new smallest block is within the site's cap.

Round after round the same two positions return, and the pile shrinks; hence the side taking whole blocks takes the last stone.

Fact 3. If the smallest block is within your cap, taking whole smallest blocks wins the pile. If it is beyond your cap, the pile is lost.

04 The lost piles

A Fibonacci pile is a single block, and the opener may take any number but not all: a cap one stone short of the block. That is the losing side of Fact 3.

Every other pile has two blocks or more, so its smallest block is within the opening cap. The opener takes it, and wins.

Fact 4. The Fibonacci piles are lost the moment the game starts; every other pile falls to the opener who takes the whole smallest block.

05 Back to the pile

The site plays Fact 3 and nothing else. When its cap covers the whole pile, it takes everything; when its cap covers the smallest block, it takes that block whole; otherwise it takes a lone stone and waits for a slip. Flip back to Play: a pile of 20 opens with a take of 2.

Who wins a pile of 100, and with what opening take? And when two piles sit on the table and each take comes from one of them, which pairs are lost?

The game is Fibonacci Nim, first analysed by Michael Whinihan (1963); the winning strategy runs on Zeckendorf's theorem.
