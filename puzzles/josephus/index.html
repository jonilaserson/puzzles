josephus · counting circle · a visual proof

Count around the circle:
who is left standing?

state the whole game in one line — put the pivotal word in bold

The game. \(n\) people stand in a circle, numbered 1 to \(n\). Count around, starting at person 1: the person who says \(k\) steps out, the count restarts at 1 on the next person, and the circle tightens — around and around, skipping empty seats.

The puzzle. One person is left at the end. Predict who, from \(n\) and \(k\) alone.

Run a few circles below before flipping to the proof: keep \(k\) at 2 and grow \(n\) one step at a time — the survivors, written in a row, give the game away.

01 The record

Each added person raises the survivor's number by two; at every power of two it drops back to 1. So write \(n\) as the last power of two plus \(l\) more people:

\[ n = 2^m + l, \qquad 0 \le l < 2^m \]

Those \(l\) people raise the survivor's number from 1 by two each:

\[ \textbf{survivor} = 2l + 1 \]

\[ 13 = 8 + 5: \quad \text{survivor} = 2 \cdot 5 + 1 = 11 \]

02 The evens step out

The first \(l\) people to step out are the even ones: persons \(2, 4, \ldots, 2l\). They all fall inside the first lap, because \(2l < 2^m + l = n\). Hence \(2^m\) people remain, and the count starts at person \(2l + 1\).

03 The starter survives

In a circle of \(2^m\) people, call the person the count starts on the starter. In one lap, every second person steps out; since \(2^m\) is even, the last of them is the person just before the starter. So the next count starts at the starter again, in a circle of \(2^{m-1}\) people. That circle is even too, and so on, down to a circle of one.

Fact 1. In a circle of \(2^m\) people, whoever starts the count survives.

The eight who remain in \(n = 13\) are such a circle:

Person \(2l + 1\) starts the count in the circle of \(2^m\) that remains. Hence:

Fact 2. For \(k = 2\), write \(n = 2^m + l\) with \(0 \le l < 2^m\): the survivor is person \(\boldsymbol{2l + 1}\).

04 One rotation

Write \(n\) in binary: the leading 1 is the \(2^m\), and the \(m\) digits after it spell \(l\). Call that block of \(m\) digits \(\ell\):

\[ n = 1\,\ell \]

Doubling a binary number shifts its digits one place left; adding 1 then fills the freed last place:

\[ 2l + 1 = \ell\,1 \]

\(n\) and the survivor share the same digits — hence: rotate \(n\) one digit left.

05 Any count

No formula like this is known for any other count. But when person \(k\) steps out, the \(n-1\) people who remain are playing the same game; only their numbers are wrong: the count restarts at person \(k+1\), not at person 1. So renumber: the person the count restarts on becomes 1, the next person 2, and so on around. In the new numbers this is a fresh circle of \(n-1\) people, same game, same count.

The fresh circle's survivor is also the survivor of the full circle, and his old seat is his fresh seat plus \(k\), wrapped around the circle of \(n\). So, writing \(s(n)\) for the survivor's seat in a circle of \(n\) people:

\[ \begin{aligned} s(1) &= 1 \\ s(n) &= \bigl( (s(n-1) + k - 1) \bmod n \bigr) + 1 \end{aligned} \]

The \(-1\) and \(+1\) convert seat numbers \(1, \ldots, n\) to remainders \(0, \ldots, n-1\) and back, so the mod can wrap.

Set the count to 2 and the recurrence retells the record: each added person pushes the seat up by two, and every wrap drops it back to seat 1 — the resets at the powers of two. Why does a wrap land exactly on seat 1?

06 The base of a climb

The graph is made of long straight climbs, in which the seat rises by \(k\) at every step, separated by sudden falls — the wraps. In this section, count the seats from 0 instead of 1: write \(S = \text{seat} - 1\). Then the recurrence loses its \(-1\) and \(+1\) and becomes

\[ S(m) = (S(m-1) + k) \bmod m \]

During a climb, every step adds exactly \(k\) to the seat. So the difference between \(km\) and \(S(m)\) stays the same for the whole climb, and the climb obeys a single formula:

\[ S(m) = km - D \]

The constant \(D\) is the base of the climb.

When does this formula break? A seat must stay below the circle size, so the formula fails as soon as

\[ km - D \ge m, \quad \text{that is,} \quad (k-1)\,m \ge D \]

The first such size is \(m = \lceil D/(k-1) \rceil\), where the bars \(\lceil\ \rceil\) mean rounding up to a whole number. There the climb wraps. What happens then? The seat drops by \(m\), so the base rises by \(m\):

\[ D' = D + \left\lceil \frac{D}{k-1} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{(k-1)D + D}{k-1} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{kD}{k-1} \right\rceil \]

The wrap lands on seat \(km - D' = (k-1)m - D\). Because \(m\) is the first size with \((k-1)m \ge D\), this landing seat lies between 0 and \(k-2\). For \(k = 2\) the only possibility is seat 0, which is seat 1 in the old numbering — the answer to the question above.

Fact 3. Start with \(D = k\), the base that puts the circle of one at \(S(1) = 0\). While \(D \le (k-1)n\), replace \(D\) by \(\lceil kD/(k-1) \rceil\). The survivor sits at \(S = kn - D\), which is seat \(\boldsymbol{kn - D + 1}\) in the old numbering.

For \(k = 3\) the rule is: add half of \(D\), rounded up. That much Josephus could do standing in the cave, with no circle and no counting — one number in his head:

\[ 3,\ 5,\ 8,\ 12,\ 18,\ 27,\ 41,\ 62,\ 93 \ \text{— past } 2 \cdot 41 = 82 \text{, stop:} \quad \text{seat} = 3 \cdot 41 - 93 + 1 = \mathbf{31} \]

Nine numbers for forty-one men: the chant needs about \(\log n\) wraps, while the recurrence walks all \(n\) circles.

Only the rounding keeps this a chant instead of a formula. When \(k = 2\) nothing is ever rounded: \(D\) simply doubles, running \(2, 4, 8, 16\) — the boxed powers of the record — and the chant collapses back into the formula of section 01. For any larger count, nobody knows how to predict the roundings without performing them, one wrap at a time.

Named for Flavius Josephus, who — the story goes — stood in a circle of 41 rebels counting off every third man, and talked his way out of the whole affair by knowing where to stand.
