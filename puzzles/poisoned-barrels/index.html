poisoned wine · one night of tasting · a visual proof

A thousand barrels,
and two of them kill

An assassin has poisoned two of the king's thousand barrels. The poison shows nothing for thirty days, then kills within hours — and the feast is in thirty-one. Ten prisoners, one night of tasting.

The setup. A thousand barrels of wine, numbered 0 to 999. An assassin has poisoned exactly two. One sip is fatal: silent for thirty days, deadly on the thirtieth.

The move. The king writes each of his ten prisoners a drink list. Tonight every prisoner sips from every barrel on his list; the night before the feast, the poison collects its dead.

The puzzle. Reading the deaths, the king serves only barrels that cannot be poisoned. One poisoned barrel is a famous riddle — ten prisoners can name it exactly. With two: how many barrels can the king promise the feast?

01 One barrel, ten digits

Warm up with the famous riddle: one poisoned barrel. Write each barrel's number in ten binary digits and stand the ten prisoners under them, A under the first digit, J under the last. Each prisoner sips every barrel that shows a 1 above him.

Every barrel leaves its own pattern of dead. One barrel thrown, 999 served.

Fact 1. Ten prisoners name the one poisoned barrel among a thousand.

02 Two barrels blur the digits

Now poison two barrels — call them the guilty pair. A prisoner dies if either guilty barrel is on his list, so the deaths show the two numbers merged: every 1 of either.

No cleverer drink lists escape this. Count what ten prisoners can say against what they must say:

death patterns ≤ 210 = 1024   ·   guilty pairs = 1000·999/2 = 499,500

Patterns are outnumbered 488 to one; hence some pattern fits hundreds of guilty pairs, and no lists can say which is true.

Fact 2. No ten drink lists can always name both poisoned barrels.

The feast, though, needs no names — it needs safe wine. Call a barrel suspect if some guilty pair that fits the deaths contains it. The king throws every suspect and serves the rest; good drink lists leave few suspects.

One drinker per barrel — ten groups of a hundred — already does well. One dead prisoner: both guilty barrels are on his list, 100 suspects. Two dead: one guilty barrel in each group, 200 suspects. The king promises 800 barrels.

Two drinkers per barrel promise more.

03 Barrels become edges

Two drinkers, and a drawing: ten dots for the prisoners, and each barrel becomes an edge — the line joining its two drinkers. Ten dots offer only 45 edges, so barrels must share; the barrels on one edge are suspects together or cleared together. Call them the edge's cell.

The guilty pair sits on two edges — or one — and kills their ends: two, three, or four prisoners. When can the deaths point at two different pairs of edges?

If the drawn edges close no triangle and no square, no second reading exists: the deaths point at one pair of edges, and the suspects are exactly its cells.

Fact 3. No triangle, no square: the deaths name the guilty cells.

04 Ten dots, fifteen edges

How many edges can ten dots carry with no triangle and no square? Call two edges meeting at a dot a cherry. A cherry's outer ends are never themselves an edge — that closes a triangle — and no two cherries share the same outer ends — that closes a square. Hence the non-edge dot-pairs carry all the cherries, one each at most:

cherries ≤ 45 − edges

A dot with d edges holds d(d−1)/2 cherries. Suppose 16 edges: the degrees sum to 32, and even their evenest split — eight dots of degree 3, two of degree 4 — holds 8·3 + 2·6 = 36 cherries (an uneven split holds more). But 45 − 16 = 29. Sixteen edges are impossible; fifteen are not:

Fact 4. Ten dots, no triangle, no square: at most 15 edges. The Petersen graph has 15.

05 The feast

Assign the thousand barrels to the Petersen graph's fifteen edges: 67 barrels in every cell of the outer ring and the inner star, 66 in every spoke's. Each prisoner drinks his three cells — about 200 barrels.

The deaths name the guilty cells (Fact 3), and the king throws at most two of them:

thrown ≤ 67 + 67 = 134  →  served ≥ 1000 − 134 = 866

Fact 5. Two poisoned barrels, ten prisoners: the king serves 866 barrels, certain of every glass.

A kindness, too: at most four prisoners die. The binary lists of the warm-up, handed the guilty pair 511 and 512, kill all ten.

Could different lists beat 866? All 499,500 guilty pairs split among at most 1024 patterns, and a pattern with t suspects fits at most t(t−1)/2 of them:

1024 · t(t−1)/2 ≥ 499,500  →  some pattern leaves t ≥ 32 suspects  →  served ≤ 968

Between 866 and 968 the answer is unknown — not just to this page; to everyone.

Two questions to take away. With more prisoners, cells could shrink to single barrels: a graph with no triangle, no square, and a thousand edges names the guilty pair exactly. How few dots can carry it? And if the assassin poisons three barrels — which shapes must the drawing avoid then?

Tasting in batches is group testing, invented in 1943 to screen army blood samples by the poolful; with two culprits instead of one, the best possible schemes are an open problem to this day. The Petersen graph is older — drawn in 1898, and turning up wherever a tidy conjecture needs a counterexample.
