dobble · finite geometry · a visual proof

Every two cards share
exactly one shape

state the whole game in one line — put the pivotal word in bold

The deck. Each card shows \(k\) different shapes, and every two cards in the deck share exactly one shape — never none, never two.

The puzzle. For a given \(k\), find the largest deck that can exist — and count how many shapes it uses. The Dobble sold in shops plays this with \(k = 8\).

Spot a few pairs below, then try building a deck of your own before flipping to the proof.

01 A deck that never ends

Break the puzzle first. Put a star on every card, and fill the rest of each card with shapes never used before. Any two cards share exactly the star — a legal deck, and it never has to stop.

It is also a dead game — the answer is always the star. One rule bans it, and it is the only rule the proof will need:

No single shape on every card.

02 Cards are lines

Now read the rule out slowly: every two cards share exactly one shape. Geometry has been saying this sentence for two thousand years: every two lines share exactly one point. Same sentence, different nouns. Lines obey Dobble's rule by nature — so let them deal. Draw lines, no two parallel, no three through one point, and drop a shape on every crossing. Each line reads as a card: the shapes sitting on it.

Free decks, straight off the page — but stingy ones: \(n\) lines make \(n\) cards of \(n - 1\) shapes each, so for a fixed card size \(k\) the drawing stops at \(k + 1\) cards. You may well have beaten that in the builder. Packing more cards means putting one shape on three, four, many cards — concurrent lines, the one thing the drawing avoided. How far can concurrency go?

03 The fan

Run \(r\) cards through one shared shape 🐸 — as lines, a fan of \(r\) blades through one point. On paper \(r\) is unlimited. But a fan is the banned cheat in a costume: if 🐸 sat on every card, that is the star again. So some card misses 🐸. Draw it — card \(D\), one more line. \(D\) crosses every blade, and no two blades at the same point: two blades meeting on \(D\) would share that shape and 🐸. So the blades hand \(D\) one shape each, all different — and \(D\) only carries \(k\).

No shape sits on more than \(\boldsymbol{k}\) cards.

04 Count from one card

Take any card \(C\) in any legal deck. Every other card shares exactly one shape with \(C\), so every other card hangs from exactly one of \(C\)'s \(k\) shapes. And each shape of \(C\) carries at most \(k\) cards — \(C\) itself and \(k - 1\) others. Hence:

A deck holds at most \(\boldsymbol{1 + k(k-1) = k^2 - k + 1}\) cards.

\[ k = 3 \to 7 \qquad k = 4 \to 13 \qquad k = 8 \to 57 \]

05 Where parallels meet

Can a deck reach the bound? Only if nothing sags — every shape of every card sitting on exactly \(k\) cards. The page itself is what resists: infinitely many points to waste, and parallels everywhere. Two repairs.

Make the plane finite. Keep one \(q \times q\) window of shapes, with \(q\) a prime, and tile the page with copies of it. A straight line on the tiled page meets the same \(q\) window shapes over and over; collapsed into the window, it wraps.

Give parallels a meeting point. Lines of equal slope still never touch — but painters have always known where parallels meet: the horizon. Hand each slope one new shape, sitting on all \(q\) of its lines. The verticals get one too, and the \(q + 1\) horizon shapes together form one last card: the horizon itself.

Every card now carries \(q + 1 = k\) shapes. Different slopes cross in exactly one window shape (a prime \(q\) keeps the wrap-around division honest), equal slopes share only their horizon shape, and every line meets the horizon card at its slope. Count: \(q\) slopes \(\times\) \(q\) shifts, plus \(q\) verticals, plus the horizon —

\(\boldsymbol{q^2 + q + 1 = k^2 - k + 1}\) cards, on \(\boldsymbol{k^2 - k + 1}\) shapes. The bound is reached.

06 All fifty-seven

\(q = 7\) gives \(k = 8\): 57 cards on 57 shapes. The pairs you spotted in Play were dealt from this very deck. (The Dobble in shops prints 55 of the 57.)

Cards and shapes coming out equal is no accident. In a full deck every shape sits on exactly \(k\) cards, so count card–shape pairs twice:

\[ 57 \text{ cards} \times 8 \text{ shapes each} = (\text{number of shapes}) \times 8 \text{ cards each} \]

Swap the words card and shape and every statement on this page stays true — geometry calls it duality.

With \(k\) shapes per card: at most \(\boldsymbol{k^2 - k + 1}\) cards, and a largest deck uses exactly \(\boldsymbol{k^2 - k + 1}\) shapes. For \(k = 8\): 57 and 57.

07 The missing k's

One loose end: the recipe wants \(q = k - 1\) prime. Powers of primes work too, with a fancier wrap-around arithmetic (\(k = 5\) runs on \(q = 4\)). But nothing covers every \(k\) — and for \(k = 7\) nothing can: no full deck of \(q = 6\) exists, so 43 cards is unreachable. \(q = 10\) fell too, to one of the heaviest computer searches ever run. Whether the bound is reachable for any \(k - 1\) that is not a prime power remains an open problem.

Meanwhile the builder is still open. The bound says \(k = 3\) ends at 7 cards: find the seven. Then look at what you built — can you point at its grid, and at its horizon?

Dobble (Spot It!) ships \(k = 8\) with 55 of the possible 57 cards. Decks meeting the bound are the projective planes of finite geometry; \(q = 6\) was ruled out by Tarry (1900) and Bruck–Ryser (1949), \(q = 10\) by Lam, Thiel & Swiercz's computer search (1989). \(q = 12\) — \(k = 13\) — is anyone's guess.
