Fifteen Balls Always
End at 1·2·3·4·5

Take one ball from each pile and make a new one. Repeat.

Split 15 balls arbitrarily into piles. You are allowed to make the following move: Collect one ball from each pile and put them in a new pile on the right. Discard any piles that become empty. Prove that no matter how you start, by applying this move you would always end up in the same state.

01 The board

The piles, standing side by side, are already a board. Count piles from the right — where new piles land — and heights from the table.

Give each ball a depth: its \(\text{pile} + \text{height} - 1\). Balls of equal depth form one diagonal of the board — diagonal \(\boldsymbol{d}\), with exactly \(d\) places on it.

02 The move, drawn

Now make a move on the board. The balls it collects are exactly the bottom row — one from every pile — and the loot stacks as the new rightmost pile: the ball from pile \(k\) lands at height \(k\). No other ball moves.

Standing balls keep their depth too: the board gained a pile on the right and lost its bottom row, so a standing ball's pile number grew by one and its height shrank by one.

Fact 1. As long as every pile is alive, a move changes no ball's depth.

03 Gaps cost depth

There is one wrinkle. When a pile loses its last ball, its place becomes empty. If the empty place is not the leftmost, the next move leaves a gap in the new pile. The balls above the gap fall to close it — and each fall costs a ball one depth.

Now add up the depths of all 15 balls and call the sum the total depth.

Fact 2. A move that closes no gap keeps total depth unchanged; a move that closes gaps drops it by at least 1.

Total depth is a positive whole number — it cannot drop forever.

Hence, after some move, no gap ever closes again, and every ball keeps its depth from that point on.

04 Ball meets hole

With no more gaps to close, every move cycles each diagonal one step: each ball slides one place down its diagonal, and the bottom ball — the collected one — wraps around to the newest pile. Holes (empty places) cycle along with the balls.

Now suppose diagonal \(d\) is not full — it has a hole — while a ball cycles the next diagonal, \(d+1\). The ball is collected — lands in the newest pile — once every \(d+1\) moves; between collections, the hole makes a full lap of its \(d\) places, plus one step. Hence each collection finds the hole one place closer, and within \(d\) collections they land together: a ball at height \(d+1\), a hole at height \(d\) — a ball resting on a gap. Impossible. Therefore the hole never existed.

Fact 3. A ball on diagonal \(d+1\) forces diagonal \(d\) to be completely full.

05 The count

Let \(D\) be the deepest diagonal holding a ball. Fact 3, applied to that ball and chained downward, fills the entire board beneath it: diagonals 1 through \(D-1\) are all full. Full diagonals hold \(1+2+\cdots+(D-1)\) balls, and diagonal \(D\) holds between 1 and \(D\) more. Now count:

\[ \begin{aligned} D &\le 4: \quad \text{at most } 1+2+3+4 = 10. \ \text{Too few.} \\ D &\ge 6: \quad \text{at least } 1+2+3+4+5+1 = 16. \ \text{Too many.} \\ D &= 5: \quad \text{diagonal 5 holds } 15 - 10 = 5. \ \text{Full.} \end{aligned} \]

Hence all five diagonals are full: the board is the exact staircase — the piles \((1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\). A full diagonal cycled one step looks exactly the same, so every move from here on reproduces the board unchanged. Frozen.

06 Playground

Watch whole games play out on the board — any start, or type your own, with any number of balls. To keep the picture calm, the drawing holds every ball in place and moves the frame instead: each move, a new pile lands on the right and the floor rises one row, collecting the balls it passes along their diagonals.

07 Why 15?

The number 15 entered the proof exactly once: the count works because \(15 = 1+2+3+4+5\). Numbers that can be written as \(1+2+3+\cdots+k\) are called triangular numbers. Can you predict what happens when the total number of balls is not triangular? Make a prediction, then test it in the playground above.

The game is known as Bulgarian solitaire; the theorem was proved by J. Brandt (1982) and popularized by Martin Gardner (1983). The board, the depths, and the gap argument on this page are due to Jonathan.
