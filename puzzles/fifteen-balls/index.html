bulgarian solitaire · monovariants · a visual proof

Fifteen balls always
end at 1·2·3·4·5

Split fifteen balls into groups — any way you like — and repeat one simple move. Wherever you start, you are pulled to the same five groups, and trapped.

The game. Take 15 balls and split them into groups, any way you please: (5, 3, 7), one big group of 15, fifteen groups of one — all fair starts. Then repeat a single move, forever: take one ball from every group, and pile the collected balls together as a new group. A group that runs out of balls simply disappears.

The puzzle. Play from any start and you will always arrive at the groups (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) — where the move recreates exactly the same five groups, so the game freezes for good. Prove that this happens from every possible start.

Really: play a few rounds below (or grab 15 coins) before flipping to the proof. The puzzle is more fun once you've felt the pull.

01 The board

Tip each pile onto its side, so every group becomes a row of balls, and stack the rows: a board.

Give each ball a depth: its row + column − 1. Balls of equal depth form one diagonal of the board — diagonal d, with exactly d places on it.

02 The move, drawn

Now make a move on the board. The balls it collects are exactly the first column — one from every group — and the new group lines up as a new top row: the ball from row r lands at column r. No other ball moves.

Standing balls keep their depth too: the board gained a row on top and lost its first column, so a standing ball's row number grew by one and its column number shrank by one.

Fact 1. As long as every group is alive, a move changes no ball's depth.

03 Gaps cost depth

There is one wrinkle. When a group loses its last ball, its row becomes empty. If the empty row is not the last one, the next move leaves a gap in the new top row. We close the gap by sliding the balls after it to the left — and each slide costs a ball one depth.

Now add up the depths of all 15 balls and call the sum the total depth.

Fact 2. A move that closes no gap keeps total depth unchanged; a move that closes gaps drops it by at least 1.

Total depth is a positive whole number — it cannot drop forever.

Hence, after some move, no gap ever closes again, and every ball keeps its depth from that point on.

04 Ball meets hole

With no more gaps to close, every move cycles each diagonal one step: each ball slides one place leftward along its diagonal, and the left-most ball — the collected one — wraps around to the top row. Holes (empty places) cycle along with the balls.

Now suppose diagonal d is not full — it has a hole — while a ball cycles the next diagonal, d+1. The ball surfaces — reaches the top row — once every d+1 moves; between surfacings, the hole makes a full lap of its d places, plus one step. Hence each surfacing finds the hole one place closer, and within d surfacings they surface together: a ball at column d+1, a hole at column d — a top row that has a gap. Impossible. Therefore the hole never existed.

Fact 3. A ball on diagonal d+1 forces diagonal d to be completely full.

05 The count

Let D be the deepest diagonal holding a ball. Fact 3, applied to that ball and chained downward, fills the entire board beneath it: diagonals 1 through D−1 are all full. Full diagonals hold 1+2+⋯+(D−1) balls, and diagonal D holds between 1 and D more. Now count:

D ≤ 4: at most 1+2+3 + 4 = 10. Too few.

D ≥ 6: at least 1+2+3+4+5 + 1 = 16. Too many.

D = 5: diagonal 5 holds 15 − 10 = 5. Full.

Hence all five diagonals are full: the board is the exact staircase — rows of 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, which is the groups (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). A full diagonal cycled one step looks exactly the same, so every move from here on reproduces the board unchanged. Frozen.

06 Why 15

The count is the only place the number 15 entered the proof — and 15 = 1+2+3+4+5 is exactly what the staircase holds. Give the game 14 balls instead and everything runs the same until the count: diagonals 1 through 4 fill up, taking 10 balls, and the remaining 4 balls cycle around diagonal 5 forever without ever filling it. The game ends in a cycle instead of freezing. A frozen state exists exactly when the ball count is 1+2+⋯+k — try it in the playground below.

The game is known as Bulgarian solitaire; the theorem was proved by J. Brandt (1982) and popularized by Martin Gardner (1983). The board, the depths, and the gap argument on this page are due to Jonathan.
