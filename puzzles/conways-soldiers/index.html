conway's soldiers · peg jumps · a visual proof

An endless army,
and a line to cross

Below the line, a soldier on every square, forever. One kind of jump moves them. Each row above the line is harder to reach than the last — climb as high as you can.

The game. Below the line stands an endless army: one soldier on every square. A soldier may jump horizontally or vertically over a soldier right next to it, landing on the empty square just beyond — and the soldier jumped over leaves the board.

The puzzle. Send a soldier as high above the line as you can. You may use as many soldiers and as many jumps as you like. How high can a scout get?

Row 1 is a warm-up; row 2 takes planning. Climb below before flipping to the proof.

01 Four climbs

Rows 1 to 4 can be reached — replay the climbs here, each made by a small crew of soldiers below the line.

Fact 1. Rows 1 to 4 are reachable — crews of 2, 4, 8, and 20 suffice.

Row 5 looks like one more push. It never comes.

02 The score

Fix a target square T on row 5. Score every square: T scores 1, and each step away — one square along a row or column — multiplies the score by σ, a number we choose below. A soldier d steps from T scores σd; a group of soldiers scores the sum.

In a jump straight toward T, the jumper starts d+2 steps from T, vaults a neighbor at distance d+1, and lands at distance d. The board loses the first two scores and gains the third. We choose σ to make that trade even:

σd+2 + σd+1 = σd

Divide by σd: every d asks the same condition.

σ² + σ = 1, hence σ = (√5 − 1)/2 ≈ 0.618

03 No jump gains

A jump runs along three squares: the jumper’s, the vaulted neighbor’s, and the landing square. Square to square, the distance to T changes by exactly one; it falls until the jump passes T’s row or column and rises after — it never rises and then falls. Hence three patterns:

Fact 2. No jump raises the score: toward T it breaks even, every other jump drops it.

Try it — every jump prints its arithmetic.

04 The army, in full

What is the total score of our entire infinite army? Sum it column by column.

The soldiers in T’s own column stand 5, 6, 7, … steps from T. Sum the column with the geometric series, then use 1 − σ = σ² (σ² + σ = 1, rearranged):

σ⁵ + σ⁶ + σ⁷ + ⋯ = σ⁵/(1−σ) = σ⁵/σ² = σ³

One column aside, every soldier stands one step farther, so the sum is σ times as much; outward, the column sums run σ³, σ⁴, σ⁵, … Columns pair off left and right:

σ³ + 2(σ⁴ + σ⁵ + σ⁶ + ⋯) = σ³ + 2σ⁴/σ² = σ³ + 2σ²

And σ² + σ = 1 finishes it:

σ³ + 2σ² = σ(σ² + σ) + σ² = σ + σ² = 1

Fact 3. The infinite army scores exactly 1, the score of a lone soldier on T.

05 The wall

A game lasts finitely many jumps, so the soldiers that ever jump or are vaulted form a finite crew. Its score starts below 1: the army scores exactly 1, and every soldier left out keeps a positive share.

Jumps trade scores only inside the crew, so by Fact 2 its score never rises. But a scout standing on T scores 1 by itself. Hence no play lands a soldier on T — and T was any square of row 5.

Theorem. Row 5 is out of reach — forever, by any finite play.

Could a jump from row 4 skip straight to row 6? Aim T at a square on row 6: every soldier stands one step farther, and the army scores only σ, still short of the 1 a scout needs.

06 A golden footnote

The number σ hides a celebrity:

1/σ = 2/(√5 − 1) = (1 + √5)/2 = φ, the golden ratio

And nothing in the score cared where T sat. Aim it at row 4: every soldier stands one step closer, every score divides by σ, and the army scores φ ≈ 1.618, yet a climb must still gather 1. What do the best sixteen squares below the line score, and why could sixteen soldiers never reach row 4?

Conway's Soldiers, devised by John Conway (1961). The proof of how high a scout can get — and no higher — is his.
